How it started

It started with a question.

Most recognition ends up in a drawer. Plaques go unread. Certificates yellow. Awards collect dust on shelves that no one looks at anymore. Not because the people they honor weren’t worth remembering — but because the objects meant to do the remembering weren’t built to last.

That question — what does a piece of recognition look like that actually endures? — is the one Eyekonika was built to answer.

The answer turned out to be light captured inside glass. Not applied to the surface. Not printed or painted on. Engraved inside the crystal itself, where nothing can reach it.

“Not on glass.
Inside it.”

The work

Subsurface laser engraving uses high-precision femtosecond lasers to create thousands of fracture points beneath the surface of optical K9 crystal — up to 200,000 per minute, each calibrated to a fraction of a millimeter.

The result is an image that exists inside the material. You can run your hand across the glass and feel nothing. The engraving is protected by the crystal itself — unreachable from the outside world. It only becomes visible when light finds it.

That’s not a marketing line. It’s physics. And it’s what makes these pieces fundamentally different from anything else in the market.

The reason

These aren’t decorations.

Every donor wall is a promise kept — to every person who gave. Every memorial is a name preserved, a moment of significance made tangible. Every dedication piece is proof that what happened here mattered, and that the people who made it happen won’t be forgotten.

We make things for institutions because institutions carry memory across generations. A church. A nonprofit. A hospital. A university. The organizations doing the most meaningful work in the world are the ones with the most to preserve — and the fewest tools that truly rise to the occasion.

That’s the gap Eyekonika exists to fill.

The founder

Matthew Kurr

Matthew founded Eyekonika on a straightforward premise: that the organizations doing the most meaningful work in the world deserve recognition that reflects that meaning — not generic plaques and printed certificates, but something genuinely permanent.

The shift to precision glass engraving came from a direct observation: that the institutional recognition market was full of interchangeable products, and that no one was building something that actually lasted.

Eyekonika is his answer to that.
